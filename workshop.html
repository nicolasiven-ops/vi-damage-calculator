Thirteen proposals for an analysis tool

The calculator plays the combo out on a clock, which means it knows more than it shows: when every hit lands, what weakened it, when an ability is ready again, how much health is left. Nearly everything here already exists as a number — it just has no form yet.

So these are not color studies but tools: each one answers a question you really do have while building a combo, and each one says underneath what it would take to get there.

About the numbers. Everything the app already computes today is shown here for real: Vi at level 11 with no items, target with 2,100 HP and 90 armor, combo Q (fully charged) → AA → E → AA → R → AA, 550 damage out of 1,005 raw. Numbers for analyses that do not exist yet — item contributions, the time-to-kill matrix, the build comparison — are plausibly invented and marked with invented numbers. No proposal here claims to be calculating anything yet.
01

The combo as a timeline, not as a list

data available

To my mind this is the most important proposal on the page. The simulation is a timeline — but the combo is built as a row of cards and read as a table, so you have to work out what happens simultaneously yourself. Here every ability sits in a lane of its own: blocks are cast times, diamonds are hits, and the thin strip under each lane is the cooldown. You can see at a glance that W's armor reduction only takes hold from 1.99 s — so it applies to R at 3.20 s, but not to the basic attacks before it. This one has since been built: the Gantt timeline is no longer a proposal but a shipped feature, and the app draws it straight from the span data the simulation already produces.

Real values from the current simulation. The lanes make visible what the table only asserts: Q charges for 1.25 s before anything happens at all, and its cooldown runs from the cast — the dash is already inside it.

The same view when the combo has to wait

The real payoff shows up in a combo that does not come together. Q Q Q is not a burst combo but three casts spread over 15.75 seconds — in the list they look identical, here they do not.

02

What is ready right now?

data available

One line that fits into the pinned bar and keeps pace while you build the combo: at the end of the combo Q still has 3.35 s of cooldown left, E has one of its two charges, R is gone for the next 111 s. That way you know before the next step whether it is possible at all — instead of finding out only from the idle time in the timeline.

04

Where half the damage disappears to

data available

The mitigation chain is the most carefully written piece of code in the project — and the most invisible one. “Throughput 55%” does not say where the other 45% went. Read as a waterfall it turns into a calculation you can follow: 1,005 raw, of which armor eats 455, and whatever W's armor reduction wins back gets a line of its own inside it.

Raw damageall hits, before anything else 1,005
− Armor 90×0.526 on physical −476
+ Armor reduction from W−20% from 1.99 s +21
Effective damagewhat arrived at the target 550

With penetration, lethality or flat reduction there would be one more row each — in the order the engine applies them. That is what turns the waterfall into a set of instructions for what to change about the build.

06

What each item really contributes

invented numbers one run per item, without it

An item advertises stats; what is interesting is the damage it actually adds in this combo. That can be answered exactly, by running the simulation once without each item and taking the difference. Out of that comes the number that carries build decisions: damage per 1,000 gold. And it exposes items whose stats never arrive here at all.

◆ Black Cleaver +189 61 / 1k g
◆ Sundered Sky +117 38 / 1k g
◆ Trinity Force +102 29 / 1k g
◆ Sterak's Gage +32 10 / 1k g
◆ Guardian Angel +6 2 / 1k g

The same view works for runes and for individual stats (“what do the last 10 AD get me?”). What would matter is an honest note wherever a passive is not modelled yet — otherwise a pure-stats item looks worse here than it really is.

09

Where the next item is still worth it

invented numbers engine across one axis

Damage over bonus AD is almost linear on Vi — the interesting part is not the slope but where the curve crosses the kill threshold, and where W's armor reduction draws a kink into it. One axis, one run per point, and there is the answer to “what is more AD even getting me at this point”.

10

Typing a combo instead of clicking it

UI only

Anyone working through twenty variants clicks six cards together twenty times. Typed out, a combo is a single line: q1.25 aa e aa r aa. The card field stays exactly as it is — the line is just a second way in, and because it can be copied it doubles as the format in which you pass a combo on to someone else.

› q1.25 aa e aa r aa
aaBasic attack — attack timer 1.32 s
wpassive, cannot be cast
wait 2wait 2 s (lets W's counter expire)
igniteIgnite — 250 true damage over 5 s
qstill 3.35 s on cooldown — would cost 3.35 s of idle time

The suggestion list is the real trick: it knows the simulation state and says, before you type, what a step would cost.

1 versus 1 — both sides on one clock

Your idea, and it is less of a gimmick than it sounds: the engine already simulates one side on a clock, and a duel is two of them on the same one. What comes out of it is the question that really counts in game — not “how much damage do I deal” but who dies first. The answer hangs on precisely the things this calculator takes seriously: idle time, cooldowns, order.

Layout

14

What separates a calculator from an analysis tool

the plan

Everything below this line is one bet: a damage calculator becomes interesting the moment it stops reporting and starts answering. "1,882 damage" is a fact nobody asked for. "Two more points of AD and the combo kills a 1,900 HP ADC" is a decision. Five principles, and every proposal after this is judged by them.

  1. Answer questions, do not display numbers. The user came with a decision — which item, which rank, which order. A number they have to interpret themselves is homework, not an answer.
  2. Solve backwards, not just forwards. Anyone can evaluate a build. The valuable direction is the inverse: how much of a stat do I need for this to kill? The simulation is cheap enough to run two hundred times and search for that.
  3. Show causality, not correlation. A total hides why. Every number should be traceable to the hit, the formula and the patch it came from — the app already labels its sources, so it can afford to be asked "why" at every level.
  4. Make comparison free. Insight comes from the difference between two runs, not from one run. If comparing costs clicks, nobody compares.
  5. Be honest about the edges. An expected value with no spread pretends crits are a constant. Say what is assumed, and say where the assumption stops holding.

The pills on each proposal say what it costs: data available needs only code, invented numbers needs a value nobody publishes, the plan is prose.

15

Solve for the stat, not for the damage

data available

The question is never "how much damage does this do". It is "what do I need for this to kill". Fix the target, fix the combo, and let the tool search the one number you can still buy: how much bonus AD, how much lethality, how many levels. The simulation runs in under a millisecond, so a binary search over two hundred runs is free.

Kill Squishy (1,900 HP · 65 armor) with the current combo needs 1 of these
Bonus AD +18
Lethality +11
Champion level +1
Ability haste never

"Never" is the useful answer for haste: no amount of it makes this combo kill, because nothing in it is waiting on a cooldown. A row that reports impossibility is worth as much as one that reports a number. Implementation: bisect on one field of bonusStats between the current value and a ceiling, calling simulate each step, stop when the target's health crosses zero. Roughly 8 iterations per row.

16

Which stat is worth most right now

data available

The same +10 of a stat is worth wildly different amounts depending on what is already in the build and what the combo actually presses. Vi's Q scales on bonus AD, her E on total AD, her W on neither — so the answer changes with the order of the steps, not only with the items. This is the derivative of the result with respect to each stat, measured rather than reasoned.

+10 bonus AD +41
+10% armor pen +29
+10 lethality +22
+10% attack speed +9
+10 ability power +4
+10 ability haste 0

Attack speed scoring low is the interesting part: this combo has two attacks in it, so faster attacks change the clock but not the damage. Add a third auto and the row jumps. Implementation: one simulate per row with the stat nudged, difference against the baseline. Six rows, six runs, once per build change.

17

Damage per 1,000 gold

data available

An item is not good because it adds damage; it is good because it adds more damage than the same gold spent elsewhere. Every purchasable item can be tried in the free slot and ranked by what it adds to this combo against this target, divided by its price. That is a ranking no wiki can give you, because it depends on your combo.

1 Youmuu's Ghostblade3,000 g +63 /1k
2 Serylda's Grudge3,200 g +51 /1k
3 Black Cleaver3,000 g +47 /1k
4 Sundered Sky3,100 g +35 /1k
passives not modelled count as stats only

The footer is not decoration. Half of these items have a passive this calculator does not simulate, and a ranking that hides that is worse than no ranking. Implementation: one simulate per candidate — a few hundred runs, so debounce it and run it in an idle callback, or restrict it to a class filter.

18

Where the next skill point goes

data available

Levelling order is one of the few decisions in the game that is pure arithmetic, and it is usually taken from a guide. For a given combo the answer is calculable: put the point in Q, in W, in E, run the combo three times, compare. It also changes with the target — W's percentage of maximum health is worth more against a tank than another 20 base damage on Q ever is.

Q Vault Breaker rank 5 → 6 not possible —
W Denting Blows rank 4 → 5 +31
E Relentless Force rank 3 → 4 +20
R Cease and Desist rank 2 → 3 +26

Against a 3,600 HP tank the W row roughly doubles and the R row halves. Showing the recommendation without the target attached would be a lie of omission. Implementation: four runs with one rank bumped.

19

The best order of the steps you already have

data available

Order changes the result — that is the premise of the whole simulation. So the tool should be able to search it. Take the steps in the bar, try every legal permutation, respect cooldowns and charges, and report the three best. Six steps are 720 orders; at a millisecond each that is a blink.

yours QAAERAA 1,882 —
best QAAAAER 1,974 +92
2nd RAAAAEQ 1,951 +69
fastest kill EQAARAA 2.71 s −0.55 s

Two objectives, because they disagree: most damage and fastest kill are different orders, and which one matters depends on whether anyone is shooting back. Implementation: permutations with early pruning, and a warning when a permutation only wins by ignoring a cooldown the simulation lets you break.

20

Can you even pay for this combo?

data available

Every cost is in the game data already — the bin file carries mana per rank next to the damage, and the app cross-checks it against Data Dragon on load. Nothing uses it. A combo that needs 340 mana from a 250 mana pool is not a combo, and right now the tool would report its damage without a word.

Q 80 E 44 R 100 641 / 865 left

Same shape for energy and for the manaless: a champion who pays nothing gets an empty rail rather than a missing one, so both sides of the workbench stay the same height. Implementation: add mana to the simulation's per-step snapshot, read the cost with the existing spellTiming(…, 'cost', rank) helper, and warn when it goes negative.

The attack timer, made visible

data available

Attack speed is the least intuitive number in the game: it does not make damage bigger, it makes the gaps smaller, and the gaps are where combos are won. Draw the cycle. Wind-up in one tone, the locked remainder in another, dead time in a third — and mark every reset, because a reset is the only thing that removes a gap entirely.

0 s1 s2 s3 s
wind-up locked dead time reset

The dead stretch in the middle is the finding: 1.02 s where the combo is waiting on a cooldown with an attack available. That is an auto nobody threw. Implementation: the simulation already records spans; this is a second lane over the same data.

22

What the reset actually bought

data available

Vi's E resets the attack timer, and every guide says to weave it between autos. Nobody says how much that is worth in this build. Run the combo twice, once with the reset honoured and once without, and the difference is the answer — in damage and in seconds.

Combo as built1,882 in 3.26 s
Same steps, reset ignored1,882 in 3.94 s
The reset is worth 0.68 s · +21% DPS

The damage is identical and the time is not, which is exactly the point: a reset buys tempo, not damage, and tempo only turns into damage if the fight lasts. Implementation: a flag on the simulation input that suppresses resetsAutoAttack, and one extra run.

Buffs and debuffs are the part of a fight that is invisible in a table of hits. The armour shred lasts four seconds; the keystone lasts three attacks; the shield lasts three seconds. Whether the ultimate lands inside or outside those windows is worth more than any total, and it is a question only a picture answers.

25

Crit is a coin, not a constant

data available

The expected value is the right number to compare builds with, and the wrong number to plan a kill with. At 40% crit chance and three attacks there are eight outcomes; "1,882" is the average of them and happens in none. Show the band, and show where the kill threshold falls inside it — that is the difference between "this kills" and "this kills 62% of the time".

kill
1,540p10 1,882median 2,310p90

Kills in 62% of rolls · needs the third auto to crit

Exact, not sampled: with a handful of crit-capable hits the distribution is a small binomial, so it can be enumerated rather than simulated a thousand times. Implementation: run the simulation in never mode, collect the crit-capable instances, enumerate the 2^n outcomes with their probabilities. Falls back to sampling above ~16 attacks.

26

Damage inside a window you choose

data available

"Burst in one second" used to be a tile here and it was removed for a good reason: nobody chose that second. But the question behind it is real — how much lands before the enemy can react, before the support arrives, before the shield expires. So let the window be dragged, and report what is inside it.

0.60 s → 1.72 s 1,204 damage 64% of the combo · 1,075 DPS

Snapping matters more than smoothness: the handles should click to hit timestamps, because "between these two autos" is the window people actually mean. Implementation: two numbers of local state and a filter over analysis.curve. No new engine work.

28

What the patch did to your build

data available

This is the one thing this tool can do that almost nothing else can. It reads Riot's own spell formulas per patch and it can hold two patches at once. So it can answer the question every patch day raises — not "what did the notes say", but "what happened to my build" — and it can answer it for patches the notes forgot to mention.

16.15→16.16 +43 total
QVault Breaker base 100 → 120 +20
WDenting Blows per 100 AD 3.0% → 3.5% +18
RCease and Desist ratio 90% → 90% 0
Umbral Glaive lethality 18 → 15 −5

Numbers invented for the mockup; the mechanism is not. The parser already reads any patch from 15.1 onwards and validates its reading against Data Dragon, so this is two loads and a diff of the resolved formulas. Implementation: fetch both patches' game data, resolve every value the champion module asks for, diff, then run the combo twice to price each change.

29

How much of this number is assumed

data available

The app already labels every value with its source. What it does not do is add that up. A result that rests entirely on Riot's own formulas deserves more confidence than one where a third of the damage comes from hand-maintained constants — and the user cannot tell which they are looking at without opening the inspector and reading twenty rows.

82% 7%
game data 1,543 Data Dragon 207 maintained constant 132

The 132 is Hail of Blades' on-hit damage — the only number in this combo that no CDN publishes in machine-readable form.

One bar, and it turns the inspector's honesty into something you can read in a glance. Implementation: tag each damage instance with the source of the formula that produced it — the champion module already knows, it just discards it after computing.

30

What was wasted

data available

Overkill is the most actionable number in a burst combo and it is currently a footnote on one tile. 487 damage of overkill means a step could have been left out, or spent on someone else. Broken down by step it becomes a decision: the last auto contributed nothing but its animation.

used 1,395 overkill 487
Cease and Desist (R) 341 of 440 wasted
Basic attack #3 146 of 146 wasted
Combo without the last two steps still kills · 0.62 s sooner

The bottom row is the payoff: the tool proposing a shorter combo that still kills. Implementation: walk the curve backwards, drop trailing instances while the target still reaches zero, then report the shortest prefix that kills.

31

From what health does this kill

data available

In a game you never meet a full-health target. What you need is a threshold you can read off an enemy health bar mid-fight: below this much, the combo kills. And the same for the shorter versions of it — because "R plus one auto" has its own threshold, and that is the one you use when you are out of everything else.

Full combo ≤ 90%
Q → E → AA ≤ 54%
R → AA ≤ 31%
One auto ≤ 9%

Deliberately expressed in percent of maximum health, because that is what an enemy health bar shows. Implementation: bisect on target.currentHealthPercent for each prefix of the combo — the same solver as the breakpoint finder, pointed at a different field.

32

Ten seconds, not one combo

data available

A burst combo is one question; a drawn-out fight is another, and the answer flips. Over ten seconds cooldowns start to matter, ability haste stops being worthless, and the auto-attack filler between abilities becomes most of the damage. Let the tool loop the combo instead of playing it once.

combo 1 autos combo 2
Burst DPS (one combo)578
Sustained DPS (10 s)412
+10 ability haste over 10 s+38 DPS

And there is the answer to the haste row that scored zero in section 16 — it was zero for a three-second burst, not for a fight. Implementation: a repeat mode for the combo that fills gaps with autos while cooldowns run, capped by a duration instead of by a step count.

34

The target's shape, not its health

data available

Effective health was a tile and it was cut, because one number against "this damage mix" answered nothing. The useful version is the profile: how much this target is worth against pure physical, pure magic, pure true damage and against your actual mix. That is what tells you whether the answer is a different item or a different champion.

vs physical 4,032
vs magic 2,982
vs true 2,100
vs your mix 3,894

Reading it: this target is 92% armour and 8% everything else, so your physical combo is fighting its strongest side. Magic damage would be worth a third more per point. Implementation: the mitigation chain already computes this; it needs four calls instead of one.

35

What the target gets to do

invented numbers

Every combo has gaps, and in a real fight the enemy acts in them. This is the bridge to the duel: mark the stretches where the target is free — not stunned, not airborne — and the tool stops describing a punching bag. It also prices the crowd control honestly: Vi's ultimate is 1.3 s of knockup, and that is 1.3 s of free damage.

knockback 0.75 s free 1.4 s knockup 1.3 s free 2.3 s

The target has 3.7 s of freedom inside a 6.2 s combo. A flash out lands in the first free window.

The invented part is not the durations — those are in the game data, and the app already reads the knockup — it is what an opponent does with a gap. So the honest version shows the windows and refuses to guess the reaction. Implementation: crowd-control spans from the same source as the buff lanes, inverted.

36

What you changed, and what it did

data available

Half an hour into a session nobody remembers which of eleven changes was the good one. The tool watches every change already — it re-simulates on each of them. Keeping the deltas turns fiddling into a record, and the record is the actual output of an analysis session.

−12 min Slot 2: Umbral Glaive +124
−9 min Keystone: Hail of Blades +96
−6 min Combo: moved R before the autos −72
−4 min W rank 4 → 5 +31
net +179 this session

The undo button is the point: a log you can act on beats a log you can read. Implementation: a ring buffer of { label, build, totalMitigated } pushed on every state change the app already funnels through one setter, coalesced so a dragged slider is one entry rather than forty.

What a stat is worth against what it costs

data available invented numbers

Sections 16 and 17 are halves of one tool. Sixteen measures what a stat does for this combo; seventeen divides damage by gold. Put them together and the question stops being “which stat is strongest” — that one has an easy, useless answer — and becomes which stat is underpriced for the combo I actually press. The shop sells stats at a fixed rate. Your combo does not value them at that rate. The gap between the two is the whole game of building.

The rate does not have to be looked up, either. Riot prices every stat through its cheapest single-stat component, and those prices are in the patch data the app already downloads: a Long Sword is 350 g for 10 AD, so AD costs 35 g a point on this patch, and the number moves when Riot moves it. Nothing here needs a hardcoded table.

“Per 1,000 g” is damage this combo gains for a thousand gold spent on that stat alone. “vs shop rate” compares it to the build’s average — above the line the stat is underpriced for you, below it you are paying retail for something your combo barely uses. Lethality reading −56% against 90 armour is the kind of answer nobody guesses right.

Where it goes from there

The next item, ranked

Every stat has a measured worth; an item is a bundle of stats with a price on it. Multiply and sum and you have the honest answer to the only question a shop screen ever asks: what should I buy next? — ranked by damage per gold, over the build that is actually on screen.

  • Serrated Dirk +63 per 1,000 g
  • Noonquiver +51
  • Caulfield’s Warhammer +44
Six slots, not infinite gold

Gold is one constraint and slots are the other. The same table sorted by damage per slot is a different order, and late in a game it is the order that matters: a 3,000 g item that beats a 1,300 g one per slot is the right buy even though it loses per gold.

The curve, not the point

A derivative is one point on a curve. Sweeping a stat from +0 to +100 shows where it bends — where crit overtakes AD, where penetration stops paying because the target has no armour left to remove. Break-even points are more useful than rankings: they survive the next item.

Shop-efficient versus combo-efficient

Two numbers per item, on two axes. The classic gold-efficiency figure says whether an item is worth its price in the abstract; the measured one says whether it is worth it to this combo. The interesting items are the ones the two disagree about — and those are exactly the ones a build guide cannot tell you about.

Implementation. The derivatives are one simulate per stat, which the app already does in milliseconds. The prices come out of item.json: filter to items whose stat block has exactly one entry, divide price by amount, keep the cheapest per stat. Stats with no single-stat component — armour penetration, omnivamp — have no shop rate, and the table should say so rather than invent one. The item ranking is the same loop over the purchasable set, and the sweep is the same loop with a range instead of a nudge.

What was scrapped

decided, not undone

Every one of these was built, looked at, and taken out again. They are here because the reason is worth more than the code was: without it the same idea comes back in six weeks wearing a different name, and gets built a second time. Each line says what it was, why it went, and what stands there now.

  • The segmented target health bar One bar divided into the hits that emptied it. Out: a health bar is a health bar. The segments made a picture that needed a legend, and the timeline already says which hit did what. A plain bar that drains, plus the translucent stub for the focused step, says it without teaching anyone a new shape.
  • The Stats window in the middle column A third panel under the analysis showing the focused moment's numbers with the change from the step before. Out: the two stat sheets in the sidebars were showing the build while it showed the moment — the same question with two answers on one screen, and the better answer was the one in the corner. The sheets now read the moment and carry the deltas.
  • “In force right now” A list under each stat sheet of the buffs, shreds and marks still ticking, with their remaining seconds. Out: everything in it is already in the numbers above it and on the timeline below it. Three places for one fact, and the panel grew to hold the third.
  • The “wait” chip A combo step that spent a chosen number of seconds doing nothing. Out: a combo is what you press. The clock between presses is the simulation's job, and an idle step is a way to fake a timing the engine already derives. Stored combos that still contain one keep working.
  • Footnotes inside the Items panel “Stats only — the passive is not modelled”, listed under the item grid. Out: the same sentences were in the notes panel at the bottom of the sidebar. Two copies meant reading it twice, and a panel that grew with the build was exactly what knocked the two sidebars out of alignment.
  • Explanatory prose in the sidebars Paragraphs under the target's stats, the crit modes and the timing constants. Out: they explained controls that say what they do, and each one cost thirty to seventy pixels on one side only — which is how a pair of mirrored sidebars stops being a pair.
  • The armour footnote and the focus caption “58 before shred · 6.3 as it is met”, and a line naming which moment the sheet showed. Out: the arrow beside the value already says it moved, the combo strip already shows which step is focused, and the timeline says what moved it.
  • Rune row labels and the “9 selected” badge A caption per rune row and a count in the panel header. Out: sixty-four pixels of caption was the difference between four keystones on one line and two. The rows are self-evident in place, and nobody was counting.
  • The ability list with names Five rows, each an icon, a name and a strip of rank pips. Out: 222 px of sidebar for what the client says in 58. Five icons in a row with the points under them, and a click that cycles the rank, is the same information and the same gesture as the game.
  • Four tiles: Burst (1 s), Throughput, Effective health, Damage instances Summary numbers across the top of the analysis. Out: “burst” answered a question about a window nobody chose, and the timeline shows when damage lands far better. “Throughput” was an invented word for a thing League already names. The last two lost their place to the verdict tile, which answers the question the page exists for.
  • A warning for a trailing E “E on its own deals no damage — add an attack after it.” Out: the warning was correct and useless. If a combo says E, it means the empowered attack, so E now carries that attack itself.

Kept as a list rather than as mock-ups: what matters is the sentence after the dash, not the thing that was removed.
